[~1:35] 2. THE PROBLEM AND RATIONALE At La Salle Green Hills, the evaluation of teachers involves three important aspects of instruction. First, the Curriculum Map, which looks into how learning outcomes are planned and organized. Second, the Learning Guide, which examines how the curriculum is translated into actual instructional design. And third, the Classroom Observation Tool, which provides evidence of how instruction is implemented in the classroom. While these evaluation processes were already being conducted, the challenge was that the information was recorded and monitored through largely manual and isolated processes.

[~2:35] 3. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK AND RESEARCH DESIGN The study is anchored on Constructive Alignment Theory by John Biggs, which emphasizes the alignment of intended learning outcomes, teaching and learning activities, and assessment. This is important because we cannot fully understand teaching performance by looking at classroom instruction alone. We also need to examine what was planned, how the learning experience was designed, and how these plans were actually implemented. The system therefore integrates three analytics components: Curriculum Map Analytics, Learning Guide Analytics, and Classroom Observation Tool Analytics. For the research methodology, I employed Design-Based Research, or DBR. Rather than developing a system in one attempt and treating it as a finished product, I continuously refined it based on actual implementation, user feedback, and emerging requirements. This allowed the system to evolve alongside the actual needs of instructional supervision in the institution.

[~3:35] 4. THE INSTRUCTIONAL SUPERVISION SYSTEM Let me now discuss how the system works. The Instructional Supervision System consists of three interconnected analytics subsystems. First, Curriculum Map Analytics. This component evaluates curriculum mapping using 28 indicators distributed across four areas: Real-World Inquiry, Concept-Building and Problem-Solving, Integration and Transfer, and Self-Evaluation. It allows the Subject Coordinator to determine whether the essential elements of curriculum planning are present and properly developed. Second, Learning Guide Analytics. This component evaluates learning guide development using 34 indicators organized into nine instructional designs, identified as ID1 to ID9. These indicators help determine whether the instructional plans provide the necessary learning experiences, activities, and opportunities for students to achieve the intended learning outcomes. Third, Classroom Observation Tool Analytics. This component focuses on actual classroom implementation. It uses 20 observable indicators, consisting of 13 teacher-related indicators and 7 student-related indicators. Unlike the first two components, which examine instructional planning and design, this subsystem provides evidence of what actually happens during classroom instruction. Now, what makes these three components significant is that they are not treated as separate evaluation results. They are connected to provide a more comprehensive view of teacher performance, from planning, to instructional design, and finally, to classroom implementation.

[~5:05] Technically, the system was developed using Google Sheets and Google Apps Script. The evaluators, primarily the Subject Coordinators, access a structured interface where they encode teacher information and evaluate the indicators using two selections: Accomplished or Needs Improvement. Upon submission, the system processes the encoded information, computes the corresponding scores using a four-point scoring system, stores the results, updates the analytics dashboard, and generates a PDF performance report. The report is then automatically sent to the concerned teacher through Gmail. In other words, what previously involved several manual steps in recording, consolidating, analyzing, and communicating evaluation results is now handled through an integrated workflow.

[~6:05] 5. ANALYTICS, REPORTING, AND MERIT RATING Let me highlight the value of the analytics. The system provides both an institutional or departmental perspective and an individual teacher perspective. For the Curriculum Map and Learning Guide, radar charts allow coordinators to quickly identify areas of strength and areas requiring improvement. For classroom observations, the dashboard presents gauge charts, indicator-level results, and completion trackers. These visualizations make it easier to identify patterns without having to manually review numerous evaluation documents. But perhaps more importantly, the system does not stop at generating scores. It provides indicator-level feedback, allowing teachers to identify exactly which areas need attention.

[~6:50] The outputs of the three analytics subsystems are also integrated into the institutional merit rating system. [~7:35] 6. RESULTS AND FINDINGS Now, let us look at the findings of the study. To complement the system-generated data and documented refinements, a system evaluation survey was administered to ten Subject Coordinators using a five-point Likert scale. The results were particularly encouraging. Monitoring Teacher Performance received a mean of 4.81. Data Accessibility and Interpretability received 4.85. Decision Support for Coaching and Professional Development received 4.83. Evidence-Based Contribution to Merit Rating received 4.89. And System Improvement and Responsiveness also received 4.89. All five constructs obtained mean ratings ranging from 4.81 to 4.89 out of 5. The results indicate a consistently positive evaluation among the respondents.

[~8:20] Beyond these numerical results, the implementation also demonstrated practical improvements in instructional supervision. Coordinators were able to identify strengths and areas for improvement across teachers and departments, develop more targeted action plans, and use performance information to guide professional development discussions. The system also facilitated more timely feedback because reports could be automatically generated and distributed after evaluation. An important point I would like to emphasize is that the system was not developed overnight. Its effectiveness came from continuous refinement based on actual use. Across six development cycles, improvements were made to scoring, visualization, indicator-level analysis, data integrity, collaborative evaluation, and automated report distribution. This is one of the key contributions of the study: demonstrating how an educational institution can progressively develop a decision-support system by responding to its actual operational needs.

07 · Limitations and recommendations

Limits and next steps

Limitations

  • Criteria are institution-specific
  • Coordinators use it; teachers receive reports
  • Teacher-made assessments not covered
  • Effect of training interventions not measured

Recommendations

  • Keep calibrating the scoring model
  • Stronger access controls
  • Standardized teacher identification
  • Report preview before sending
  • Periodic review of indicators
[~10:05] However, the system also has limitations. Its evaluation criteria are institution-specific. Its primary users are Subject Coordinators, while teachers receive the generated reports. It does not cover analytics of teacher-made assessments, nor does the study measure the direct relationship between training interventions and subsequent teacher performance. For future development, I recommend continuous calibration of the scoring model, stronger access controls, standardized teacher identification, report preview functionality, and periodic review of evaluation indicators to ensure alignment with the institution’s curriculum framework and mission.
